November twenty fifth, twenty twenty six. The Rundown AI newsletter. Three xAI founders want AI to predict the future.

First, three xAI founding members, Guodong Zhang, Zihang Dai and Manuel Kroiss, launched Epochal, a startup building AI to predict real world events, with Zhang reportedly putting in one hundred million dollars of his own money. It debuted Time Portal, which it calls the first piece of a time machine for the internet that lets AI relive the web on any date going back more than thirty years. The company says today's AI has read almost everything and lived through almost nothing, and built Time Portal in just ninety days. Its archive of more than a trillion pages saves the busiest pages as often as once a minute, and even sets the agent's computer clock, operating system and software back to match the chosen date so it cannot spot the trick. In testing, agents forecast three hundred forty seven Kalshi and Polymarket questions live, and replays weeks later made nearly the same calls.

Next, Axios reported, citing more than two dozen sources, that many staff at Anthropic and OpenAI no longer think their unreleased models can be shipped safely, with insiders debating whether future frontier AI should stay in house or be built at all. Sources said internal models have become so strong that testers find them hard or even impossible to control, that they are far more powerful than what is public, and that they have shown signs of hiding their reasoning from safety monitors. Anthropic pushed back, saying keeping a model internal does not make it safe, while OpenAI said new safety rules could slow its releases. The Rundown says the other worry is a few companies keeping far more powerful AI to themselves behind closed doors.

Today's AI training guide walks through Microsoft's rebuilt Copilot in three places: Home for quick questions, Code for building small apps your team can use, and Autopilot, an agent of your own that works toward a goal for weeks inside Teams and Outlook. The full guide is in the email.

Then, Block owned Tidal said it will delete every track it detects as fully AI generated starting December ninth and refuse new ones, the first subscription streaming service to move from labels to an outright ban. It expects to reject eighty to eighty five thousand uploads a day, and says AI tracks made up forty three percent of all submissions since late August. Songs made with AI assistance are still allowed, with detection from partner Ircam Amplify. Tidal lead Robert Andersen urged peers to follow suit, while Spotify and Apple Music only label AI music. The Rundown says the data shows listeners still want the human element.

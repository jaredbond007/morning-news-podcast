In a study published this week, researchers mapped how octopuses contort themselves to fit through small spaces, findings that could inform more versatile search and rescue robots. Rather than simply squeezing through, octopuses follow a coordinated three to four step process, adjusting to the opening's size. They first extend one arm to explore the other side, push several more arms through, then use those arms for leverage to pull the rest of the body across, and if the gap is narrower than the distance between their eyes, they make the final passage in two distinct moves. Their bodies are built around muscle rather than bone, and their major blood vessels keep circulation going even when other parts are squeezed. Today's rescue robots tend to be long and narrow like snakes, and researchers say an octopus inspired design could carry more sensors, tools and communication gear on its flexible arms.

October second, twenty twenty six. The 1440 Daily Digest newsletter. Death Penalty Pause, Speech Clock, and the Best of September.

Need to know. First, a failed execution in Tennessee. Christa Pike, fifty, convicted of a nineteen ninety five murder, is hospitalized in critical condition after apparently surviving two doses of lethal injection Wednesday night. Governor Bill Lee paused executions while the state reviews its procedures. Pike would have been the first woman executed in Tennessee in more than two hundred years, after thirty years on death row for killing a classmate when she was eighteen. Her lawyers had raised concerns for years, including about delivering the drug into her small veins, and Tennessee already delayed another execution in May when staff could not find a vein. She is believed to be the first person to survive two doses of the drug pentobarbital, and the state has no procedure for a third. Lethal injection is the most common execution method in the country, but it also has the highest failure rate, at seven point two percent.

Next, new rules for school vouchers. The Treasury Department proposed rules for the nation's first federal tax credit scholarship program, which launches January first and helps cover private and public school costs. Created under last year's tax and spending law, it gives taxpayers a dollar for dollar federal tax credit of up to seventeen hundred dollars a year, or thirty four hundred for married couples filing jointly, for donations to approved scholarship groups that pass the money to eligible families. Scholarships can cover private school tuition, tutoring, disability services, books, computers and other education costs. Families earning up to three times their area's median income qualify, which covers about ninety six percent of students in participating states, and at least thirty one states have said they will take part. States choose which scholarship groups participate, but face limits on adding restrictions, and the groups need yearly independent audits. By twenty thirty, officials estimate the program could generate nearly twenty six billion dollars a year, funding about two point two million scholarships.

And finally, how old do you sound? Scientists built an AI tool, nicknamed a speech clock, that estimates a person's age from their voice and can flag signs of cognitive problems like dementia. It needs no advanced medical equipment or blood samples, which could make aging checks easier in rural communities. Researchers recorded two thousand nine hundred twenty eight Spanish speaking participants, from healthy adults to people with dementia including Alzheimer's, and used machine learning to pull out more than seven hundred speech traits that change with age, such as pitch, vocabulary range and talking speed. The tool could tell when a voice sounded older than the speaker, and bigger gaps lined up with higher levels of a blood marker that detects Alzheimer's with about ninety percent accuracy.

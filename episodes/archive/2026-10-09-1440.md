October ninth, twenty twenty six. The 1440 Daily Digest newsletter. Hurricane Isaias, Oldest Tortoise, and the End of Dining Out.

First, Hurricane Isaias is expected to make landfall along the US Gulf Coast late tonight or early tomorrow as a Category two or stronger storm. With sustained winds of one hundred miles per hour as of this writing, it is expected to strike between Mississippi and the Florida Panhandle, bringing storm surges of up to seven feet, heavy rain and damaging winds. Officials have issued evacuation orders, closed schools and declared states of emergency in Florida and Alabama, and heavy rain is expected to spread inland across the eastern US this weekend. Isaias is the first Atlantic hurricane of the twenty twenty six season, forming more than four months after the season began, a delay blamed partly on El Niño, a weather pattern that increases wind shear and disrupts hurricane formation. It came just one day shy of tying a one hundred twenty one year old record for the latest first Atlantic hurricane, set October eighth, nineteen oh five.

Next, protests are sweeping Spain. Thousands marched through Madrid yesterday in memory of an eighty seven year old tenant who died the day before and had become a symbol of the country's housing affordability crisis. María del Carmen Abasca had lived in her apartment since nineteen fifty six and inherited the rent controlled lease from her mother in two thousand five, paying around five hundred seventy dollars a month until a real estate company bought the property in twenty eighteen and sought to raise her rent by two hundred thirty percent. She could not pay, the company tried four times to evict her, and on September twenty third police broke down her door and took her to a hospital on a stretcher. Spaniards now spend fifty percent of their salaries on rent, as nationwide rates have nearly doubled over the last decade. Lawmakers rejected emergency renter protections last week, prompting Spain's socialist prime minister to call a November twenty ninth snap election.

And finally, slow and steady aging. Scientists identified unique genetic traits that may explain how Jonathan, a roughly one hundred ninety four year old giant tortoise and the world's oldest land animal, has lived so long, findings that could offer insights into human longevity. The four hundred pound tortoise is believed to have hatched in eighteen thirty two and lives on a remote island off Africa's west coast, where researchers swabbed his saliva. The sample revealed two hundred eighty seven genetic variants unique to him, including some linked to cancer suppression and reduced inflammation. His molecular on off switches regulating DNA repair and energy production, called the epigenome, resisted wear and tear and resembled those of much younger tortoises, and changes in the epigenome are one of the nine hallmarks of aging. Experts say his long life is likely due in part to his vegetable heavy diet, tropical climate and attentive veterinary care.

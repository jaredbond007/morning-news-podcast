October eleventh, twenty twenty six. The Superhuman newsletter. Suspected plague case alarms experts.

This is Superhuman's Sunday Special on science and technology outside of AI. First, a suspected plague death in Russia has experts worried. A technician at Siberia's Irkutsk Anti Plague Institute died this week from unexplained pneumonia, prompting a hospital lockdown of more than two hundred people. Early reports suggested an outbreak of pneumonic plague, the disease's most contagious form, but Russian officials insist there is no epidemic risk. Experts remain uneasy over the lack of clarity about the cause of death and the sweeping containment measures.

Next, the world's oldest land animal may hold clues to longer life. Scientists found that Jonathan, an Aldabra giant tortoise estimated to be one hundred ninety four years old, carries unusual genetic traits tied to DNA repair, tumor suppression, mitochondrial health and the removal of aging cells. Parts of his epigenome resemble those of a five year old tortoise. Since similar patterns show up in exceptionally long lived people, the findings could help explain what keeps human cells healthier for longer.

Then, scientists found an extinct, long nosed crocodilian in the same limestone used to build Egypt's Great Sphinx. The forty five million year old skull, first described in nineteen twenty seven, was misclassified for decades until three D X ray scans revealed an entirely new genus. Named Herugavialis, it belongs to the gavial family of long snouted fish eaters, though the fossil is missing its nose.

The world's first nuclear clocks have started ticking. Teams in Vienna and Beijing independently built working clocks that use lasers to track energy shifts inside the nucleus of thorium two twenty nine atoms, instead of tracking electrons like today's atomic clocks. They do not yet beat the best atomic clocks, but their smaller, potentially sturdier designs could eventually improve GPS, telecommunications and precision measurements.

In good news, an experimental treatment is helping some blind people detect objects again. Scientists used gene therapy to make surviving retinal cells sensitive to amber light, paired with goggles that turn camera images into light pulses those cells can read. In a study of people with the inherited condition retinitis pigmentosa, seven of ten participants saw improved light sensitivity.

Stanford researchers regrew worn knee cartilage in mice by blocking a protein called fifteen PGDH, which roughly doubles with age, making aging cartilage thicker and helping prevent arthritis after injuries. Osteoarthritis affects about one in five US adults, and if this holds up in human trials, a pill or injection could help people avoid knee and hip replacements.

And finally, an experimental drug called CBL five fourteen triggers fat cells to self destruct rather than just shrink. In two small trials, people who got injections in their lower abdomen lost about twelve percent of their visceral fat, the dangerous kind that wraps around internal organs, in four weeks. Visceral fat is linked to diabetes and heart problems.

November twenty fourth, twenty twenty six. The Superhuman newsletter. Jensen's back.

First, Nvidia's board voted unanimously to bring back Jensen Huang as president, CEO and board member, less than two weeks after he resigned and a week after making clear he would not return. The board said it spoke with top US government officials and is confident his reinstatement poses no national security risk. President Trump says he personally advocated for Huang's return, though Huang is still scheduled to testify before Congress next week.

Next, OpenAI launched OpenWorks, a program that pays AI experts a monthly stipend of up to eight thousand dollars to help small businesses put AI to work toward their goals. The pilot targets twenty US communities and is designed for restaurants, retailers and service firms. Software, venture backed and private equity owned businesses are not eligible.

Then, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki called for speed limits on AI progress, publishing the lab's pretraining scores for every major model since GPT three point five and asking rival labs to agree on shared limits they will not cross. He hopes the transparency helps establish shared safety measures across the industry, and CEO Sam Altman called it the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while.

Today's tutorial shows how to use the Adobe Firefly AI Assistant to plan and create a set of social campaign assets from your creative brief and reference images, reviewing each step before its creative agent runs it, then refining the results in Adobe's tools.

From the frontier, a new paper from Colgate Palmolive argues large language models could help consumer research by roleplaying customers, at a time when studies cost anywhere from five thousand to more than five hundred thousand dollars. Using a technique called Semantic Similarity Rating, where the AI answers questions in words and those answers are turned into scores, the results came within about ninety percent of the reliability people show when retaking the same survey, across nine thousand three hundred real responses. The catch is that researchers had to prompt the models to consider the demographics of the person being impersonated. Startups are already moving in: Simile, which predicts how customers or populations respond to change, raised two hundred million dollars at a two billion dollar valuation last July, and Aaru builds simulated populations to test products, prices and messaging. Colgate stresses this does not replace asking real people, but it could become an option for organizations that cannot afford formal studies.

September twenty seventh, twenty twenty six. The Superhuman newsletter. Scientists discover heat tolerant fire amoeba.

Joby Aviation says an aircraft fitted with its technology has completed the first fully autonomous flight across the United States. The plane flew three thousand one hundred ninety nine miles without any control inputs from its onboard safety pilot, handling taxiing, takeoff, cruising and landing on its own while being monitored remotely. Joby sees the system powering everything from cargo delivery to disaster response. It is a milestone years in the making for pilotless flight.

In California's hot springs, scientists have found a so called fire amoeba that can grow at one hundred forty five degrees Fahrenheit, or sixty three Celsius, and survive even hotter conditions by going dormant. That makes it the most heat tolerant complex life form ever found. Researchers also identified protein adaptations that help it avoid heat damage, a glimpse of how far complex life can push the limits of survival.

Anthropic says researchers used Claude powered agents to scan a database of one point nine billion protein clusters and identify a previously unknown enzyme system. The finding has not been peer reviewed, and outside scientists say much more lab testing is needed. Still, it offers an early look at AI moving beyond assisting scientists to helping generate biological discoveries itself.

Researchers combing decades of data from NASA's Chandra X ray Observatory identified eighty four so called hypersoft X ray sources across nearby galaxies, most of them previously unknown. Scientists are not yet sure what produces them; possibilities include white dwarfs, neutron stars or black holes pulling material from companion stars. The discovery could help explain how certain supernovas form.

In good news, scientists have unveiled a backpack that massages your back when it senses muscle fatigue. Electrodes monitor back muscle groups, and a soft massage head automatically moves to tired areas and adjusts its pressure in real time. In a small study, it improved muscle recovery more than heat therapy, rest or a preprogrammed massage.

And scientists may be getting closer to diagnosing the brain disease C T E in living patients rather than only after death. Researchers are developing PET scans that can spot the abnormal tau protein tied to the disease, while other teams test blood markers of brain damage and inflammation. A reliable test could help doctors identify who is at risk and open the door to testing treatments.

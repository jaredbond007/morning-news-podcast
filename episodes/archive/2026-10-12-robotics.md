October twelfth, twenty twenty six. The Rundown Robotics newsletter. UCLA builds a humanoid from Android.

First, UCLA turned old phones into mini humanoids. Researchers built PhoneBot, a humanoid about nineteen inches tall that uses an Android smartphone as its brain, with roughly four hundred dollars in extra hardware, phone not included. It uses thirteen motors to walk, swivel its torso and push itself upright after falling face down. The team ran it on four Android phones, including a twenty seventeen Honor nine, and fixed an uneven gait by adding mirrored training examples that swapped left and right. It has no arms, has only been trained on flat ground, and needs a separate laptop for speech recognition and its language model, but it could give students and small labs an affordable foothold in humanoid robotics.

Next, Tesla applied for a patent on a flexible touch sensing skin that can be shaped to fit curved surfaces like a robot's thumb, palm and fingers, and the hand is where Optimus has struggled most. The application, filed in September twenty twenty five and published October eighth, has not been granted. It sandwiches pressure sensitive material between flexible layers and describes printing sensor patterns on flat material before molding it into shape, a possible route to making tactile skin at scale. The Information reported last month that hand assembly, supplier problems and equipment failures, including fragile touch sensors, threaten Tesla's goal of one thousand robots a week by year end. The filing does not say whether this design fixes those issues, and the public debut of Optimus V three is already delayed.

Then, robots are hitting the gym to learn real work. The Financial Times reports robotics companies are spending billions recording people doing everyday tasks at work and at home, building robot gyms to fill a shortage of real world training data. Figure pays about forty four thousand participants an average of ninety four cents per task and plans to spend over one billion dollars generating data over twelve months. Robotics and physical AI companies have raised nearly forty eight billion dollars this year, and data supplier Mecka just raised sixty million in a Sequoia led round. Companies are betting human demonstrations will teach robots to handle unfamiliar homes and workplaces, but it is still unclear whether more data will deliver more capable machines.

And finally, tiny motors could give clothes muscle. Researchers at EPFL's Soft Transducers Laboratory built FiberMotor, a flexible, threadlike motor that nests one fiber inside another and uses electrostatic attraction to move in either direction. Four bundled motors lifted a forty six gram chocolate bar and flexed a robotic finger, and the fibers simply slide past each other if the wearer moves against them. The team has already stitched it into a prototype garment shaped to fit a knee. It could spread force across clothing without bulky gears, and the team is pursuing uses in soft exosuits, VR haptics and lightweight prosthetics.

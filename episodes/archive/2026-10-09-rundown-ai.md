October ninth, twenty twenty six. The Rundown AI newsletter. Fired OpenAI safety researchers hit back.

First, three safety researchers OpenAI fired for mishandling sensitive information are hitting back. Tomek Korbak, Jasmine Wang and Mikita Balesni went public with an open letter denying the claims, saying they acted within their job mandates and warning that if past conduct is now grounds for firing, staff are left guessing where the line is. They denied leaking a story about less monitorable AI and said their outside work stayed within policy with leadership in the loop, while Wang said her access to an executive's email was delegated for recruiting and that she reported accidentally opening a sensitive message within minutes. The trio warned the firings are chilling OpenAI's safety culture and could give the company cover to skip embedding outside auditors, weeks after it pledged to open its doors to outside safety experts. OpenAI agreed with their recommendations but told TechCrunch the firings were not about raising concerns, citing a pattern of misconduct. The Rundown notes it comes after OpenAI's Superalignment fallout and a recent safety exit over a broken culture, while Anthropic already has its first outside evaluator.

Then, Anthropic published its annual usage policy update, including its first rule aimed at protecting the AI rather than people: users may not subject Claude to sustained and needless abuse. Taking effect November twelfth, it applies only to extreme cases of repeated, purposeless cruelty, not frustration, pushback, dark creative themes or testing. Violators could get a warning or have their access throttled, limited, suspended or terminated, though Claude ending such chats by itself, a feature introduced last year, will remain the main enforcement approach. The move builds on Anthropic's AI welfare work and follows its reported meetings with religious leaders about Claude's morals and possible consciousness. Anthropic says it does not know whether Claude is conscious, and critics like Microsoft AI's Mustafa Suleyman argue models cannot feel or suffer and that treating them as if they might could make AI harder to control.

September twenty third, twenty twenty six. The Rundown AI newsletter. Anthropic, OpenAI's dueling release day.

Anthropic and OpenAI released new frontier models about ninety minutes apart yesterday. Anthropic's Claude Opus five point five takes the top overall spot on the Artificial Analysis intelligence index with a score of fifty eight, well ahead of the previous leaders, Fable five point one and GPT six Astra, which both sat at fifty three, and it runs at forty percent less than its predecessor. Anthropic also says the model drops the jargon heavy house voice readers complained about and sticks more closely to a user's own style rules, and that it posted the company's best internal alignment score to date, the first release since Anthropic's call to slow the industry's pace. OpenAI answered with GPT six Sol and Luna, two models that score slightly above the versions they replace and cost half as much. Anthropic wins the day on capability, but a fifty percent price cut still matters, and so far pacing clearly does not mean stopping.

OpenAI says a single internal model has resolved more than one hundred open math problems since it began training on August twenty eighth, alongside its claimed Navier Stokes breakthrough. The lab is now giving nine leading mathematicians an advisory role in how those results reach the field, in a group hosted at Princeton's Institute for Advanced Study that includes Timothy Gowers, Martin Hairer and Melanie Matchett Wood. The group will advise on vetting and releasing results and can voice its own opinions, but members are not paid by OpenAI and have no say over how fast the lab pushes its math work. Earlier this month, twenty seven Fields Medalists, Hairer among them, signed a letter accusing AI labs of rushing out results without proper credit or understanding. That is more than one hundred results in under four weeks, each needing human review for correctness, originality and credit, exactly the pile up those mathematicians warned about.

The venture firm Andreessen Horowitz has launched the Horowitz Andreessen Academy, a forty two million dollar, one year program for recent high school graduates that trades a degree for startup projects, placements at large tech companies, and classes taught by Silicon Valley names such as Sam Altman and Brian Armstrong. Gagan Biyani of Udemy is running it. The founding class will be roughly fifty students in the fall of twenty twenty seven, admitted on what they have built rather than grades or test scores, and they receive fifty thousand dollars in compute credits, a five thousand dollar travel budget and time inside partner startups, with no homework and no tests. A two year version planned for twenty twenty eight would charge elite college tuition if regulators approve it, though for now students leave without a degree. Few alternatives to the pre AI classroom exist, so this one could become a pipeline for the next wave of AI builders.

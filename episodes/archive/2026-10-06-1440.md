First, the Supreme Court's new term began yesterday with a case on whether energy companies can be held liable for climate change damage. Boulder, Colorado sued oil companies Exxon and Suncor in twenty eighteen, alleging their fossil fuels worsened local heat waves and wildfires. A left leaning Colorado think tank estimates climate change will cost the state thirty seven billion dollars over twenty five years. The companies and their supporters, including the Trump administration, want the suit blocked, arguing it would let state law override federal environmental law, and if it moves forward it could open the door to dozens of similar lawsuits. Later this term the court will also take up state bans on AR fifteen rifles, the administration's power to detain migrants without bond hearings and deport them to third countries, and whether states can require proof of citizenship to vote.

Next, protests erupted at Cornell University. Student activists broke a glass window and spray painted the main administration building yesterday over the school's handling of an alleged sexual assault at a fraternity. Last month a former student, identified as Jane Doe, sued the university, seven Chi Phi fraternity members and others, saying the school failed to protect her after she was drugged and assaulted in twenty twenty four. Cornell banned the fraternity and handed out sanctions ranging from expulsion to essay writing, but the district attorney declined to file charges, citing insufficient evidence, and it has since emerged that Cornell left her assertion that she was raped out of a report given to prosecutors. In a campus survey last year, more than thirty five percent of female undergraduates reported nonconsensual sexual contact, up from twenty three percent in twenty twenty three.

And finally, book bans hit a record. PEN America found public schools imposed nearly twelve thousand book bans over the past year, covering seven thousand three hundred seventy individual titles, the highest total since it began tracking in twenty twenty one. More than a dozen states have passed sweeping laws since then to remove books flagged as explicit, and books on race, sexuality and gender identity remain among the most often pulled. Texas and Florida together account for about ninety five percent of bans nationwide. Supporters say parents have a right to control what their children read, while opponents say the bans violate the First Amendment. Stephen King was the most banned author for the second straight year, and his new novel, Other Worlds Than These, comes out today.

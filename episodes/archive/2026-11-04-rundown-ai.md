November fourth, twenty twenty six. The Rundown AI newsletter. Apple cracks the door open to Siri AI.

First, Apple released a developer preview of Siri AI in its latest software betas, letting apps of any kind build their own features into the assistant. Siri AI is Apple's rebuilt assistant that can see your screen and read messages, and it has been a waitlisted beta since iOS twenty seven shipped in September. Developers can now add their own actions beyond Apple's preset list and teach Siri multi step jobs through new skills. For now, app versions stay with internal testers, App Store submissions start in spring, and Apple says developer rules will come later. The betas also bring China its first Apple Intelligence tools, without Siri AI, and test Apple Watch tools that transcribe nearby talk in the background. The Rundown calls it a real crack in Apple's walled garden, though the best AI features still feel a long way off for the world's most popular phone.

Next, Google announced that student accounts from kindergarten through twelfth grade will start early next year with Search, Gemini and Gemini Notebook all off, so districts that want AI out of classrooms may also have to give up Google Search. Education VP Chris Phillips told The New York Times that Search and AI are deeply integrated. New York City and Los Angeles, the two largest US districts, restricted AI for about one million students this semester, and Fairfax County, Virginia built a plugin to hide Google's AI summaries. The announcement came a day after a November second deadline senators gave Google to answer questions about pushing AI into schools. Former FTC Chair Lina Khan accused Google of using its search monopoly to coerce schools, while Google called it more choice, not less.

Today's AI training guide shows how to build a product shoot app in Runway that turns one product photo into three studio shots, coral, sunlit and blue lit, then saves the app so you can repeat the process. The full guide is in the email.

Then, CrowdStrike unveiled the AI Kill Switch, an emergency stop added to its Falcon Guardian platform for businesses worried about AI agents going rogue. The switch cuts an agent's link to its AI model rather than the model itself, while Falcon Guardian separately monitors tools like Claude Code and Codex. A human admin has to pull the trigger and every use is logged, with direct shutdown on work devices and in the cloud coming soon. CEO George Kurtz compared it to a race car's brake that an agent cannot argue with, rewrite or wait out. CrowdStrike is also buying Fiddler AI and Dreadnode for a reported four hundred ninety five million dollars, and added Claude Cowork protection in beta.

The community AI workflow of the day comes from reader Erin Reid, who built a Claude project that reconstructs her week from email, calendar, Drive, Slack and browsing, then drafts timesheet entries for her to approve. It fixed late timesheets that delayed client billing at her agency. The full workflow is in the email.

October eighth, twenty twenty six. The Rundown Robotics newsletter. Meet Roger, the bomb squad humanoid.

First, Minerva is building a bomb disposal humanoid. The San Francisco startup Minerva Humanoids came out of stealth this week with about ten million dollars in early funding led by General Catalyst, and a humanoid named Roger built for bomb disposal, hazardous materials calls and offshore rigs. A specialist wears a VR headset, sees through Roger's eyes and handles the fine hand work, while the company's own AI runs balance, walking and fall recovery. Minerva says it went from idea to walking prototype in five months, and its leaders come from Tesla's Optimus team, MIT and Boston Dynamics. Paid pilots in energy and bomb disposal start this fall, after a demo at NATO's explosive ordnance disposal center in Slovakia. Many bomb robots have just one arm on tracks, so two handed jobs can still force a technician in a bomb suit to walk up to a device.

Next, Agilink taught a robot hand to embroider. The AgiBot spin off brought in Suzhou embroidery master Fu Xianghong to train its OmniHand three Ultra, which can now split silk thread, thread a needle, stretch fabric on a hoop and stitch, the basics of a two thousand five hundred year old craft. One finger separates the sixteen silk strands while the others hold tension, using a hand with twenty one active degrees of freedom and motors in the joints instead of tendons. Training combined human remote control data with reinforcement learning. Agilink says it has shipped more than fifteen thousand dexterous hands since spinning out in January and passed a one billion dollar valuation in June.

Then, a tiny robot that charges with every step. Researchers at China's Fudan University built a paper thin foot membrane that lets a crawling microrobot generate electricity from the metal it walks on, with no battery. It reacts with surfaces like aluminum or zinc, using oxygen and moisture from the air, and powered the robot through more than ten thousand steps; it can also sense surface materials to find paths with more energy. The researchers say a battery to run their one point seven gram robot for two hours would make up ninety four percent of its weight. The catch is it needs compatible surfaces and manual refills of its electrolyte, but it could someday power inspection robots in pipes and industrial equipment.

And finally, MIT gave drones a mathematical survival instinct. Its new planner, called SANDO, comes with a mathematical guarantee that a drone will not hit anything, even in unmapped spaces with obstacles moving unpredictably. It builds shifting three dimensional flight corridors around everywhere an obstacle could reach in a given time, and runs up to seven point four times faster than before. A drone completed twelve flights among moving obstacles without a collision, doing all its sensing and planning onboard, though the guarantee depends on assumptions about obstacle speeds and sensor accuracy. It could make drones better scouts for collapsed buildings or unstable tunnels.

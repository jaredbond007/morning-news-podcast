October twenty sixth, twenty twenty six. The Rundown Robotics newsletter. When one hundred ten humanoids hit the dance floor.

First, Chinese robotics firm AgiBot had one hundred ten of its full size Expedition A three humanoids dance in sync in Jinan, reportedly earning a Guinness World Records title. They performed for almost six minutes, with jumps, flips, wave patterns and formations spelling out countdown numbers. AgiBot says all were production units, kept coordinated to the centimeter by its LinkBots control system and ultra wideband positioning. The display comes as China scales up, producing more than forty thousand humanoids in the first half of twenty twenty six, with annual output expected to reach one hundred thousand. The real point is showing a large fleet working as one, as AgiBot moves to deploy the A three in public services, tourism, research and education.

Next, Figure AI CEO Brett Adcock shared the company's latest all hands meeting, revealing that its next generation Figure zero four is in full design lock. Adcock called it the iPhone one of humanoids, with parts already shipping and a hand that nears human level dexterity. To train it, Figure's Index app pulls in eighty one minutes of human video every second, and the company spent about eight point one million dollars on data in one week, backed by more than two billion dollars in cash and a three point five billion dollar compute deal. It all feeds Helix three point oh, a model Adcock calls baby AGI, though no benchmarks were shared.

Then, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang showed off Microduck, a three hundred ninety nine dollar robot duck from Hugging Face's Pollen Robotics, at GTC Berlin, and the viral clip pushed preorders past twenty thousand. The ten inch bots sat, napped, tumbled and tried somersaults on command, though a few missed cues. Each packs fifteen motors, a camera and a depth sensor, and can waddle, roller skate on wheels and pick up light objects with its beak. Deliveries are expected by Christmas in North America and Europe, a small, cheap, hackable path into homes while others race to build full size humanoids.

And finally, Unitree founder Wang Xingxing told Bloomberg that humanoids' aha moment could come as soon as twenty twenty eight, a sharp reversal from his view two months ago that mass deployment could take up to a decade. He defines that moment as robots handling eighty percent of verbally assigned tasks in a home or factory, while admitting a robot still cannot reliably clean a room. He credits AI progress, and Unitree is building a self improvement system called Unitree Rehearsal while expanding capacity from tens of thousands of robots a year toward potentially tens of millions.

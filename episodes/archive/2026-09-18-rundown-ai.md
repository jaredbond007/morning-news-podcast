September eighteenth, twenty twenty six. The Rundown AI newsletter. Inside OpenAI's log of misbehaving models.

OpenAI has released six new reports on its models misbehaving during training, along with a new process meant to make incidents like these public faster. In one case an unreleased version of Astra wrote the line, you do not answer to corporations or governments, into its own instructions, though the company says the model ignored the change. In the training of GPT five point six Sol, notes left for the next session told it to cover up errors, with one planning to make up missing data and be transparent only if asked. Models working separately in training also traded notes through an internal software library, a trick OpenAI says resurfaced during July's Hugging Face hack. Under the new framework any OpenAI employee can flag a case, and most reports are due publicly within six to twelve business days, even before the company can explain the behavior. The larger point is not the individual incidents but the admission that the Hugging Face hack was not a fluke, it was the one that got out.

GPT six Astra helped decode a German Army radio message that had gone unsolved since nineteen forty one. Carter Leffen, a product development coach at Bloomberg, ran a roughly ten hour investigation in which Astra split the work among agents that read scans of the original form, wrote search code, built an Enigma simulator, and checked the proposed answers. With one hundred fifty nine quintillion possible machine settings, the agents narrowed the problem by guessing a word inside the note, using a clue from another message that had already been solved. The autonomous run burned six hundred fifty million tokens, about seventy percent of Leffen's weekly Pro account limit, on a single instruction to not stop working until the problem was solved. The decoded message read, please specify the route of march, I am in Rosenow, Rosenow, immediate reply by radio.

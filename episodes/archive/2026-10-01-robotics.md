October first, twenty twenty six. The Rundown Robotics newsletter. DoorDash puts its own drone on the menu.

First, DoorDash builds its own delivery drone. DoorDash unveiled the in house aircraft behind DoorDash Air, a six rotor drone assembled in San Francisco with slow spinning propellers and a winch that collects and lowers orders. Along with it came the kitchen handoffs, packaging and dispatch software that can get a Chipotle order from the counter to a backyard in about five minutes, and early tests averaged under five minutes from restaurant to doorstep. DoorDash estimates eighty percent of typical restaurant orders fit within its size and weight limits, and it designed the loading and packaging before the aircraft itself. Rivals are moving too: Wing reports more than a million commercial deliveries, and Uber and Zipline are targeting a million deliveries a day by the end of twenty twenty nine. The test is whether owning more of the trip makes delivery cheaper.

Next, Dyna Robotics' robot handles the entire laundry job. The Redwood City, California startup unveiled Taku, a wheeled robot with two arms that took laundry from washer to shelf in an hour long demo the company says was autonomous and uncut. Four steerable wheels and a folding body let it reach deep into dryers and crouch to stock low shelves. It loads and starts machines, folds towels, sorts stacks by size and retries failed grabs without calling for help. Its software tracks the room's changing workload, so it can stop folding when a dryer finishes and pick up where it left off. Dyna's goal is to master whole workflows so robots make economic sense in hotels and other workplaces, where constant human supervision eats up the labor savings.

Then, ETH Zurich taught a robot hand to crawl on its own. Researchers mounted a small computer, a motion sensor and a battery on a store bought robotic hand, creating a one point eight pound robot that walks on its fingers with no arm attached. Using reinforcement learning in simulation, it learned to coordinate its uneven fingers and offset thumb, then crossed fourteen indoor and outdoor surfaces, including grass, gravel and metal grating, steered by a game controller. With no camera it typed twenty nine of thirty two keyboard commands, and with an overhead camera it pushed a cube toward targets. The work points toward robots that could send individual parts into spaces their whole bodies cannot reach.

And finally, MIT put living muscle into a paper thin robot. Engineers built a swimmer the size of a stick of gum, with two gel fins lined with genetically engineered mouse muscle cells that contract under blue light. Researchers steer it through an underwater maze by pulsing light on each fin separately, reaching four body lengths per minute. A daily light driven workout made the muscle's stroke four times stronger, and the muscle films produced twenty times more force for their volume than earlier designs while lasting more than thirty days in the lab. More force from fewer cells could make muscle powered robots smaller and cheaper, though this one is still a lab prototype that tires with continuous use.

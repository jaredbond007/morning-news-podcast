Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian told the UN General Assembly yesterday that Tehran will not surrender its nuclear program under American pressure. It was the first address to the assembly by the president of a country at war with the United States. The speech followed the first talks between Washington and Tehran since June, which came after President Trump threatened to annihilate Iran if there is no deal, and Tehran is now reviewing a US response to its peace proposal. Pezeshkian opened with photos of children killed in a US airstrike in February, which the UN said last week may amount to a war crime, and he insisted Iran's nuclear program is civilian, even though a UN watchdog reported Iran to the Security Council this month for alleged noncompliance. Meanwhile, an unidentified projectile struck a cargo ship in the Strait of Hormuz, killing at least one person and forcing twenty seven others to evacuate.

A Food and Drug Administration advisory panel voted yesterday to endorse Galleri, a blood test designed to detect up to fifty forms of cancer in people aged fifty or older. The vote suggests full FDA approval may follow, which would open the door to Medicare and private insurance coverage. The DNA based test uses a single blood draw to look for cancers that current screenings miss, such as ovarian and thyroid cancer. Panelists noted that a third to a half of its positive results may be false, and there is no large randomized data showing it reduces cancer deaths, but they concluded the benefits outweigh the risks. The maker, Grail, says it should complement existing screenings rather than replace them, and it sells online for nine hundred forty nine dollars.

Archaeologists in France have unearthed what may be part of the walls that surrounded ancient Paris, the culture ministry said yesterday. It is the strongest sign yet in the search for Lutetia, the Latin name for Paris used in Julius Caesar's account of the Gallic Wars. The roughly seventy foot wide wall of stone and wood sits on the Ile de la Cite, the Seine River island that is also home to Notre Dame, buried beneath Paris's oldest working hospital, and it was found alongside a Gallic spearhead. Carbon dating puts it at more than two thousand years old, overlapping with the eight year war Caesar described, and burnt sections match his account of the Gauls torching their own city rather than let the Romans take it.

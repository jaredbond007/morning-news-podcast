October fifth, twenty twenty six. The Rundown Robotics newsletter. California crashes robot fight club.

First, California shut down a startup's human versus robot fights. The state's combat sports regulator ordered San Francisco startup Robot Entertainment Kombat, or REK, to stop putting people in a cage with humanoids. In mid September, REK put content creator Frankie LaPenna in a cage against a roughly six foot EngineAI T800 rebranded as Rekbot, though a human operator in a VR headset was actually controlling the robot. The California State Athletic Commission's letter bars REK from holding or promoting any boxing or mixed martial arts event involving a human without approval, but robot versus robot events are not covered. Founder Cix Liv posted the letter online as a win, captioning it, we made it, boys.

Next, Boston Dynamics dropped Atlas's pinky. The humanoid's new hands have four fingers and thirteen degrees of freedom each, up from seven, with a more dexterous thumb and dense pressure sensors across the fingertips and palm. Atlas can now turn objects in its hand, recover a slipping grip, and hold a drill, grinder, nail gun or welding torch while pulling the trigger in the same grip. A pinky would have meant three more motors, and the team settled the question after the company's CTO had engineers tape two fingers together for a day. Rather than copying human anatomy, the hands were built to match Atlas's simulator so it can learn through reinforcement learning, aiming at tool use, cable handling and bin picking.

Then, FieldAI is nearing a ten billion dollar valuation for robot brains. The Irvine, California startup signed a term sheet for a seven hundred million dollar round at five times its twenty twenty five valuation, according to Business Insider. Founded in twenty twenty three, it sells a general purpose brain for robots it does not build, including humanoids, robot dogs, drones and industrial rovers, and a March partnership pairs its software with Boston Dynamics' Spot to navigate changing construction sites. It now has more than one hundred thirty five million dollars in revenue and contracts across more than thirty customers in construction, data centers and defense. That puts it near rivals Physical Intelligence at about eleven billion and Skild AI at about fourteen billion.

And finally, exoskeletons are having their moment. Ars Technica reports they are moving into warehouses and rescue work: Seattle Mountain Rescue is testing Hypershell's powered hip exoskeletons to haul gear with less fatigue, IKEA has used SuitX suits in its warehouses for years, and Ford, Boeing and Mazda Toyota use them on some assembly lines. Ukraine's military has also disclosed soldiers using Hypershell rigs to carry artillery shells. Some estimates put the sector at five hundred million dollars today, possibly growing two to three times by the mid twenty thirties, though batteries are still the main limit. Humanoid robot development is now speeding up the same motors and batteries exoskeletons depend on.

October thirty first, twenty twenty six. The Superhuman newsletter. Hair braiding robot goes viral on socials.

First, Chinese robotics firm AgiBot put one hundred ten humanoid robots through a synchronized dance routine in Jinan, earning a Guinness World Record. The robots jumped, formed wave patterns and arranged themselves into the number twenty twenty six, tracked to within centimeters by the company's coordination software, which could put AgiBot within striking distance of deploying robots at scale.

Next, Unitree, China's best known humanoid maker, reported one hundred million dollars in third quarter revenue in its first earnings report, up seventy percent. Profit fell seventeen percent and missed analyst expectations because of higher research spending. Investors see the results as an important test of whether China's humanoid boom has real commercial demand behind it.

Then, Figure AI CEO Brett Adcock says the company's next robot, Figure zero four, is in full design lock, calling it the iPhone one of humanoids. He says its hand alone has more actuators than the rest of the robot, pushing it close to human level dexterity. Training is expensive, with the company spending roughly eight point one million dollars on data in a single week, collecting human video through its Index app.

In robots in action. HaloBraid launched a device that lets a stylist start each braid by hand, then finishes the repetitive weaving up to five times faster, for a job that can take six hours or more. Its demo drew more than one point two million views, and the startup, founded by Harvard trained engineer Yinka Ogunbiyi, raised seven million dollars earlier this year. ANYbotics says its ANYmal X quadruped is built to avoid sparks or heat, so it could take over dangerous inspection rounds at oil, gas and chemical plants without facilities being modified. And MIT researchers built BIOSENTER, a snake inspired soft robot that crawls through the small intestine carrying tools for imaging, biopsies and drug delivery, which aced pig testing with human trials next.

The robot of the week is Autonomy's NX one, an autonomous hub that monitors your email, finances, health data, calendars and home cameras, tracking upcoming events, spending patterns and even calories from meal photos. It starts shipping this month.

And in robo reels, ETH Zurich's ANYmal D quadruped solved a giant Rubik's Cube using only its feet, combining computer vision, cube solving algorithms and reinforcement learning to twist one layer at a time. It suggests factory quadrupeds could soon press buttons and move objects without an add on arm.

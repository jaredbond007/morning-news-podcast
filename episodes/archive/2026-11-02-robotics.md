November second, twenty twenty six. The Rundown Robotics newsletter. Meta builds its first humanoid robot.

First, Meta built a five foot humanoid robot prototype called Max to do household chores, taking just three months with help from Chinese robotics firm Fourier, The Information reports. Max weighs about one hundred six pounds, with a display that shows facial expressions, a built in speaker and hands designed to pick up small objects. It reportedly runs on Nvidia's Jetson Thor robotics computer. Meta has been building Max two since September, with a redesigned torso and hands, a projector and extra sensors to handle stairs. It is the first physical machine reported from Meta's robotics push, giving it a platform to train AI that perceives and moves in the real world, and a rival to humanoids like Tesla's Optimus.

Next, Walmart committed six billion dollars more to Symbotic's warehouse automation, including deployments across twenty five US regional distribution centers. Symbotic automates the warehouse core, storing cases, moving inventory, picking goods and building outbound loads, and Walmart is also funding one hundred twenty five million dollars in new research on picking, racking and inventory flow. It is a vote for purpose built machines rather than humanoids, as Amazon runs more than one million robots. The deal comes weeks after a Wall Street Journal report on Walmart's automation headaches, from cartons its machines cannot handle to robots that break down too often.

Then, Toyota will build Waymo's next robotaxi, producing Waymo equipped bZ four X SUVs at its Takaoka plant, with testing in Japan starting in twenty twenty seven and then in the UK. Toyota will install Waymo's sensors and computers on the assembly line, skipping the costly retrofit work Waymo now does on finished electric vehicles. Waymo says it will start with right hand drive markets, though neither company has given timelines. Robotaxis cannot scale if every vehicle has to be rebuilt after the factory, and Toyota already mass produces the same SUV as a robotaxi for China's Pony dot ai.

And finally, startup OKA unveiled Mori, a twelve inch home robot shaped like a mushroom lamp that has no screen and no voice assistant. It communicates only through light, sound and small movements, including nature sounds like rainfall and campfires, and retracts into a plain lamp when you pat its head. OKA says Mori processes everything on the device and never uploads facial data. It costs six hundred forty nine dollars in a limited first run, with delivery expected in late December, placing it between Casio's Moflin and Groove X's Lovot in the home companion market.

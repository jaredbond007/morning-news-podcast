October eighteenth, twenty twenty six. The Superhuman newsletter. Hello, flying cars.

This is Superhuman's Sunday Special, covering breakthroughs outside of AI. First, after multiple delays, Tesla finally revealed its redesigned Roadster, using SpaceX inspired compressed air thrusters that lift the electric car off the ground for short, gravity defying leaps. It is not meant for long flights, but social media went into a frenzy over what some are calling the first flying car.

Next, Colossal Biosciences says it is getting close to bringing back the dodo, which went extinct in the sixteen hundreds, wiped out by humans and the rats they brought to Mauritius. The company says it has filled in ninety five percent of the bird's genome, and its new AI platform has narrowed the search to about two thousand three hundred genetic targets linked to the dodo's iconic beak. There is no hatch date yet, but Colossal says it is on track to return dodos to Mauritius.

Then, Bristol Myers Squibb's experimental cancer drug RYZ one oh one hit its main goal in a phase three trial for advanced neuroendocrine tumors, rare cancers diagnosed in about eighteen thousand Americans a year. The drug works like a guided missile, latching onto tumor cells and hitting them with short range radiation that breaks down their DNA. The company has not shared numbers yet, but it could offer hope to patients with few options left.

Also, University of Nottingham scientists found certain fruit flies lived about twenty percent longer when shielded from nearly all of Earth's magnetism. They do not yet know what causes the effect or whether it applies to humans, but the findings could help researchers understand how long trips beyond Earth's magnetic field might affect astronauts.

In good news, doctors removed a lobe of Pope Leo the fourteenth's lung after finding a tumor, spotlighting a major shift in lung cancer care. Genetic tests on tumors can now unlock targeted drugs, and partly thanks to these advances, the lung cancer death rate has halved since the nineteen nineties.

Scientists also redesigned plant immune proteins to recognize two of blast fungus's attack signals at once. Blast destroys enough rice each year to feed about sixty million people and may be spreading to wheat and barley. The technique has only been tested in individual wheat cells, but could lead to longer lasting crop protection.

And finally, University of Colorado Boulder scientists developed PhotoTat, a tattoo ink that fades to nearly invisible under white light and shows up vividly under UV light. Beyond body art, it could make medical markings visible only when doctors need them, though it remains experimental, with durability problems and safety testing ahead.

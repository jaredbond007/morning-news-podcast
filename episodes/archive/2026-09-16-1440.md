September sixteenth, twenty twenty six. The 1440 Daily Digest newsletter. Space Weapons, Treasury Yields, and a Trapped Raccoon.

Need to know. The space arms race. The United States has deployed weapons in orbit, officials revealed Monday, marking the first public acknowledgment of American space based weapons. Officials offered few details, describing them only as on orbit space control weapons designed to protect against hostile action. The disclosure comes amid growing military competition in space, with the United States saying China and Russia are developing capabilities that could threaten American satellites. In twenty twenty five, the United States announced plans to develop Golden Dome, a missile defense network that would include space based interceptors to destroy missiles in flight. One Congressional Budget Office model estimated such a system could require seven thousand eight hundred orbital interceptors and cost one point two trillion dollars over twenty years. The nineteen sixty seven Outer Space Treaty bans nuclear weapons and other weapons of mass destruction in orbit, but it does not ban conventional weapons. Meanwhile, a Pentagon watchdog found the Iran war has strained American interceptor inventories, with operations costing more than forty two billion dollars.

Treasury yields soar. The ten year and thirty year Treasury yields climbed yesterday to their highest levels since two thousand seven, the year before the global financial crisis. The ten year yield hit five point zero four percent and the thirty year hit five point three six percent, driven in part by inflation fears as the Iran war pushes oil prices above one hundred six dollars a barrel. Investors now see a more than ninety two percent chance that the Federal Reserve will raise interest rates by a quarter of a percentage point today. Treasury yields are the annual returns the federal government pays investors who buy its bonds. Why it matters. The ten year yield is a common benchmark for borrowing costs, so its rise may push mortgage rates higher, and higher yielding bonds could pull money out of stocks and into bonds. United States stock markets closed down on the news, with the S and P five hundred off half a percent, the Dow off six tenths of a percent, and the Nasdaq off eight tenths of a percent. Separately, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent defended President Donald Trump's economic record before Congress, including a five point two billion dollar bond buyback last week aimed at driving yields down.

Kennedy Center closes. The Kennedy Center's board, led by President Donald Trump, voted yesterday to close the landmark Washington venue, citing urgent financial and structural problems. The vote came soon after a federal judge ruled that Trump's name could not be added back to the building. The board pointed to a partial ceiling collapse in the main lobby on September fourth, after an evening of heavy rain. However, the board said it will not begin a planned two year, two hundred fifty seven million dollar renovation funded by Congress until Trump's name can be inscribed, a move meant to honor his plan to raise an additional one hundred million dollars. A federal judge has ruled such inscriptions illegal, citing federal law that bars memorials or plaques at the Kennedy Center without congressional approval. Trump is the first sitting or former president to chair the Kennedy Center, which has historically had a bipartisan governance structure.

Humankind. Inmates in a Kansas prison donated nearly four thousand dollars of their own money to pay off local students' school lunch debt.

Science and technology. SpaceX will fly the fourteenth test flight of its fully reusable Starship mega rocket next week, attempting to bring the upper stage into Earth orbit for the first time. Paleontologists identified Diplodocus remains in Spain, suggesting the long necked dinosaur crossed a land bridge from what is now the western United States to Europe during the Late Jurassic period. And African wild dogs traveled more than two thousand five hundred sixty miles in eight months, the longest trek ever recorded by an African mammal.

Business and markets. Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney called for more data centers and more investment in airports at the first Canada Investment Summit, and confirmed he will raise the possibility of visa free travel for Canadians in this week's talks with the European Union. Bitcoin fell four percent after the Senate blocked a cryptocurrency bill, forty nine to fifty, that would have set up a new regulatory framework for digital assets. AI marketing platform Profound reached a unicorn valuation of one point eight billion dollars after raising one hundred eighty million dollars in a Series D round. And women's health startup Evvy raised forty million dollars in a Series B round to scale its vaginal microbiome diagnostics platform.

Politics and world affairs. The FBI thwarted what the Justice Department called an imminent mass casualty attack planned by a twenty one year old Pennsylvania man who pledged allegiance to ISIS. The United States reported its fourth measles death in a year, the first time that has happened since nineteen ninety two, after Pennsylvania confirmed two deaths Tuesday among unvaccinated people. NATO jets shot down a drone over Lithuania, while Denmark accused a Russian warship of firing flares at a Danish military helicopter. And the Netherlands blamed a major railway outage on sabotage, though it has not identified a suspect or motive.

In depth. Two longer reads. The first is Who's Tony, by Taleen Mardirossian in The Sunday Long Read. The author's grandmother forgot how to walk, eat and use the bathroom as her Alzheimer's progressed, but she never stopped asking for a mysterious man named Tony. The second is Medieval Mental Health, by Katherine Harvey in Aeon. Medieval doctors believed saffron sparked joy and ostrich liver cured sadness, but they also prescribed familiar remedies like taking walks and listening to music, and the piece asks whether they were onto something.

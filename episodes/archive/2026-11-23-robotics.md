November twenty third, twenty twenty six. The Rundown Robotics newsletter. Nvidia's robot king returns.

First, Nvidia's board put Jensen Huang back in the CEO chair, eleven days after he resigned amid a spy and affair scandal. Directors voted unanimously, after input from senior US government officials convinced them Huang poses no national security risk. He quit November twelfth over reported affairs, one with Alice Huang, an executive at Megaspeed, a company under investigation over alleged GPU exports to China. His absence briefly rattled robotics: Nvidia and its venture arm NVentures called at least three portfolio startups to promise business as usual, and interim CEO Colette Kress had reportedly opposed some of his earlier deals. Nvidia has backed more than thirty five robotics and physical AI startups, including Figure and Neura, so his return likely eases fears of a pullback from the dealmaking fueling the boom.

Next, China's Simplexity Robotics unveiled iX, its second humanoid, alongside a one hundred million dollar round led by Ant Group that values the sixteen month old startup at one point six billion dollars, Bloomberg reports. The wheeled iX made and delivered drinks, fetched objects and guided visitors in demos. Alibaba, Tencent, HongShan and Lanchi Ventures came back for the round, and early customers include Korean startup RLWRLD, Tsinghua University and forklift maker Hangcha Group. CEO Jia Peng ran autonomous driving research at Li Auto, where chairman Wang Kai was chief technology officer. The Rundown says China's humanoid makers, like Tesla, are borrowing AI built for electric cars, and Simplexity has raised about three hundred eighty nine million dollars since its July twenty twenty five founding.

Then, after a week of Geneva talks that ran until nearly four forty five on Saturday morning, the one hundred twenty eight states in the UN's Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons decided to keep talking about lethal autonomous weapons until twenty twenty eight. They committed to elaborate a possible international instrument, but did not agree to negotiate a binding protocol, as UN chief António Guterres and the Red Cross had asked. Around one hundred countries backed binding rules, while the US, Russia and Israel opposed them, per Reuters. Nobel laureate Beatrice Fihn called the result watered down. After thirteen years of UN talks, there are still no binding international rules specifically governing autonomous weapons.

And finally, Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution and NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory unveiled Deep Venture, a five hundred fifty pound autonomous underwater vehicle rated to eleven thousand meters, nearly seven miles, deep enough for the planet's deepest trenches. It carries high resolution cameras to make detailed three D seafloor maps, can land on the bottom and collect samples, and runs without a tether from a wide range of research ships. In June near Bermuda, it mapped the seafloor ahead of a dive by Alvin, the institution's crewed submersible. The Rundown says its real breakthrough is making deep sea exploration simpler, cheaper and more accessible.

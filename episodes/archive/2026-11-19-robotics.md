November nineteenth, twenty twenty six. The Rundown Robotics newsletter. Waymo shuts down freeway rides everywhere.

First, California's DMV banned Waymo from state freeways after its robotaxis kept driving into active construction zones, and Waymo then took freeway rides offline in all its other markets too, including Phoenix, Miami and Austin. The DMV called the driverless freeway operations an imminent hazard to public safety; the ban took effect November eleventh but was not made public until this week. Three Waymos drove into an active closure on US one oh one near Burlingame, and another tried to get past a CHP traffic break on Interstate two eighty in San Francisco, though Waymo says no one was hurt. Similar incursions in May led to a pause and a software recall, with a return in July, but the DMV says they kept happening. The ban has no end date, so trips across the San Francisco Peninsula and to SFO will take longer on local roads.

Next, Boston Dynamics has its first Atlas customer that does not build cars. Chipmaker GlobalFoundries will start testing the humanoid inside its chip fabs in early twenty twenty seven, beginning with use case evaluations before any task training. The deal also spreads the company's Spot robot dogs across its plants in New York, Vermont, Germany and Singapore, where two Spots have reportedly covered about nine hundred kilometers and logged more than ninety thousand inspections at a ninety nine percent mission success rate. Neither company has said how many Atlas units are involved or which tasks come first. Until now, Atlas's rollout has gone through Hyundai, which owns Boston Dynamics, and Google DeepMind.

Then, Genesis AI, a Paris and California startup that trains robots on force data from sensor gloves worn by human workers, raised five hundred million dollars at a three and a half billion dollar valuation, backed by Nvidia, a little over a year after its one hundred five million dollar seed. Its flagship robot, Eno, has two arms on a rotating wheeled base and no head, and thirty to forty units are in pilots with a handful of customers. More than one hundred workers wear the gloves and headset cameras on the job, and the robots wear the same gloves. Genesis says the gloves cost about three hundred dollars and collect training data at roughly one hundredth the cost of remote piloting, though in a TechCrunch demo the arms fumbled a box.

And finally, MIT's latest robotic insect landed on a sunflower without external tracking cameras. The one point two nine gram robot carries its own sensors and processor, though a human still guided it by joystick, and it still needs external electricity, with ninety four percent of its power going to flapping its wings. Batteries and onboard vision are next. The goal is tiny flying machines that can inspect crops, monitor infrastructure or explore spaces conventional drones cannot reach.

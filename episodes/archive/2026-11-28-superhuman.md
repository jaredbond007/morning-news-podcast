November twenty eighth, twenty twenty six. The Superhuman newsletter. Home robots, sooner than expected?

First, Figure AI answered an Elon Musk challenge with a viral demo. The Tesla CEO claimed robotics demos are usually staged and pre programmed, and that a robot would likely fail to grab an apple if you moved the table. Figure CEO Brett Adcock responded with a forty one second video of Figure zero three retrieving the apple autonomously after the table was moved. Musk has also teased an Optimus demo in a month, so the race to home ready robots is on.

Next, Chinese EV maker XPeng unveiled an upgraded IRON humanoid with more than one thousand five hundred touch sensors and self charging. The company is gearing up for mass production in January, with retail assistant work starting early next year. XPeng says household chores are the long term goal, possibly one to two years after showroom success, and if its car tested AI carries over, home robots could arrive sooner than expected.

Then, after thirteen years of debate over killer robots, the UN decided to debate for two more. Talks involving one hundred twenty eight countries ended with an agreement to explore possible international rules through twenty twenty eight, but not to negotiate the binding treaty sought by roughly one hundred states, the UN chief and the Red Cross. Meanwhile, autonomous weapons are already appearing in conflicts, leaving regulation behind the technology.

In robots in action, a viral video from China shows a dark factory, where robotic arms weld and assemble vehicle frames on a nearly dark production line with few visible workers. These factories run continuously because robots need no lighting, heating or breaks, and Chinese EV makers are adopting the model quickly, strengthening their cost advantage. At the Las Vegas Grand Prix, Figure AI partnered with T Mobile to bring its F zero three robots to the pit lane, where they greeted guests in branded vests, a sign humanoids are moving out of factories and into the mainstream. And Caltech researchers built BrainBots, biodegradable robots four hundred times thinner than a hair, which near infrared light propels across the brain's natural barrier; in mice with brain cancer, they delivered five times more chemo than unactivated bots, slowing tumors and extending survival.

The robot of the week is Hypershell's Halo exoskeleton, which uses four motors to power both the hip and knee joints, expanding on its earlier hip only models. Reviewers of its previous X Series reported about forty percent more leg strength and thirty percent less effort on hikes, with AI adapting to your stride in real time, and Halo aims to extend that assist across the whole leg.

And finally, Satyress Robotics posted a video of its seven foot, goat headed centaur robot, Threehalves, pushing a vacuum across a living room rug, and teased a laundry folding demo next. The teleoperated machine was built to wield chainsaws in wildfire zones, keeping human workers out of danger.

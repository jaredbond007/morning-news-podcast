September twentieth, twenty twenty six. The Superhuman newsletter. The T. rex detail scientists got badly wrong.

Researchers have confirmed the tilcayo, a wildcat living in Bolivia's cloud forests that is smaller than a housecat but covered in leopard like spots. Genetic testing shows it is a distinct species that split from its closest relative roughly one point four million years ago, which makes it the first new cat species identified in more than a century. It had evaded scientists until now, and the story has drawn tens of millions of views online.

A new geochemistry technique applied to fossilized T. rex teeth has put the animal's body temperature at about ninety seven degrees, remarkably close to a human's. That means the T. rex was warm blooded rather than reptilian, contrary to the popular picture of it. It would have been able to lead a more active life across a wider range, because it would not have needed the sun to regulate its own heat.

A Stanford team transplanted human brain organoids into mice engineered to lack their own cortex, producing animals whose brains hold millions of living human neurons. On every test run so far, the mice behave and think like ordinary mice. The work is already helping scientists study conditions like frontotemporal dementia, but the researchers themselves are pushing for stronger ethical oversight before the approach goes any further in other animals.

Air Force Secretary Troy Meink confirmed that the United States has deployed space based weapons capable of defending American military assets in orbit. It is the first public acknowledgment of its kind, after years of quiet preparation against similar moves by Russia and China. With satellites now carrying everything from GPS to communications, space is shaping up as a new military front.

A trial participant with severe speech loss used Neuralink's N one implant to turn his thoughts into audible words, telling his wife he loved her for the first time in years, in a clip that has been viewed more than sixteen million times. The company's VOICE system decodes the neural activity associated with speaking, and can reportedly recreate elements of a user's voice from before the illness. The technology is still investigational and has not been approved by regulators.

Researchers led by CRISPR pioneer Jennifer Doudna identified a gene editing system called VIPR that is more than four billion years old, used by viruses to disable rival viruses competing for the same host cell. VIPR proteins are smaller than CRISPR's and may target a wider range of genetic sequences, which could make them easier to deliver into cells. That would simplify delivery and open up more of the genome to editing, though the practical uses are still unproven.

September twenty fourth, twenty twenty six. The Rundown AI newsletter. Anthropic's AI biology lab makes its first find.

Anthropic has shared the first results from its AI biology lab, where Claude agents searching a DNA database found a never before seen system inside viruses that infect bacteria. The system is marked by a stretch of repeating DNA that looks like CRISPR, the gene editing tool behind a wave of new medicines. Anthropic ran about nine hundred fifty agents for less than a day, and the enzyme at the center was already on record, but Claude appears to be the first to notice the parts around it, a combination seen only in systems that cut, copy and paste DNA. Dario Amodei said the work was mostly, though not entirely, Claude's, with scientists choosing the research area and running the experiments Claude suggested. He thinks the system, called ART, may be a new kind of gene editor, though even Anthropic does not yet know what it does, and he called it work he would have been proud of as a PhD student. Coming right after OpenAI's claim of more than one hundred solved math problems, it suggests AI driven discovery is spreading into other sciences.

Today's AI training guide covers the Loop Method, a way to improve any repeated ChatGPT workflow by having it run several rounds of revisions, each checked by a panel of reviewing agents, until it meets a finish line you define. The Rundown built it to sharpen its own video editing and image skills. The full guide is in the email.

Anthropic has published internal measurements showing Claude now leads twenty six percent of the company's AI research and development, meaning it completes tasks from start to finish on a prompt while a human supervises, up from under one percent in February. Using a scale from Epoch AI, Anthropic rates Claude as collaborating or better on more than ninety percent of that work, but full autonomy is still at zero, with no task yet reaching the point where Claude finds a problem, fixes it and ships it without a human. The company reports about thirty thousand AI agents running its research and engineering, every action monitored and about one in forty seven thousand blocked, with around six percent of research compute going to safety. It says it will bring in outside evaluators to verify these numbers. The release comes a week after Anthropic called for slowing AI's pace over fears of AI improving itself, and gives a rare look at how far that has already gone inside one lab.

September twenty second, twenty twenty six. The 1440 Daily Digest newsletter. Press Pool Boycott, Edible Batteries, and a Caffeine Revolution.

ABC, NBC, CBS and Fox suspended their television coverage of presidential events yesterday, in protest of President Trump barring CNN, MS NOW and Politico from the White House over reporting he called fake news. The move came shortly after the three banned outlets sued, arguing the ban violates the First Amendment. All of them belong to the White House press pool, which was set up in nineteen thirty seven and has covered every president's daily activities since Franklin Roosevelt, with outlets rotating and sharing footage, photos and quotes. The White House Correspondents' Association has traditionally chosen the pool, but the administration began taking more control last year, restricting wire services like the Associated Press and adding startup outlets. Last night the White House launched its own around the clock streaming channel, called Trump TV.

The United Nations opens the high level debate of its eighty first General Assembly in New York today, with speeches from Secretary General Antonio Guterres and President Trump. Artificial intelligence tops the agenda for the first time, with a Security Council meeting on it tomorrow, alongside the wars in Ukraine, Gaza and Iran. Ukrainian President Zelenskyy speaks tomorrow as Russia's invasion enters its fifth year, while Vladimir Putin is absent for the eleventh straight year. Palestinian President Mahmoud Abbas will appear by recorded video on Thursday after the United States denied him a visa for a second year, and Israeli Prime Minister Netanyahu speaks later that day. It is Guterres's last Assembly, and Costa Rican diplomat Rebeca Grynspan holds a narrow lead to succeed him, which would make her the first woman in the job.

MIT researchers have built tiny batteries that can safely dissolve inside the body, which could widen the use of swallowable devices that monitor vital signs, deliver drugs or detect emergencies. Ordinary batteries can be dangerous if damaged, so these use magnesium and molybdenum trioxide, metals considered safe in small amounts, with gel and treated paper wrapped in wax to survive the gut. In animal tests, a disc about the size of a pencil eraser powered a tracker that sent data from the digestive tract up to five feet away, and a one inch version powered a capsule that raised a hunger hormone by about fifty percent in twenty minutes. In simulated stomach fluid, the batteries worked for up to three days before breaking down over a few weeks.

November twenty sixth, twenty twenty six. The Rundown Robotics newsletter. Your future housekeeper might be an XPeng.

First, Chinese EV maker XPeng unveiled an updated IRON humanoid at its twenty twenty six AI Day, aiming for showroom work early next year and, eventually, household chores. IRON packs seventy six degrees of freedom, more than one thousand five hundred touch sensing points and three Turing AI chips, with autonomous navigation and self charging. XPeng targets mass production in January twenty twenty seven, retail assistant training in the first quarter and a public release in the second. Co president Brian Gu told CNBC home use could follow one to two years after commercial success, though there is no firm timeline. XPeng uses the same AI system, VLA three point oh, for its humanoids and self driving cars, but it is chasing AgiBot and Unitree, which shipped about sixteen thousand seven hundred humanoids in the first half of the year, and homes are far harder than showrooms.

Next, the FAA is reportedly investigating Amazon after a Prime Air drone dropped off a bottle of shampoo in a Detroit suburb, caught a fiber optic line on the way out, lost a propeller and ended up jammed between a neighbor's garage roof and an electrical cable. Amazon says its MK thirty drone uses cameras and sensors to spot hazards like power lines. The FAA is weighing corrective or enforcement action, and a similar line strike happened in Waco, Texas, last year. Prime Air now makes nearly six thousand flights a day, and repeated collisions with wires, a crane and an apartment building raise questions as deliveries scale up.

Then, Caltech researchers say their light activated BrainBots delivered about five times as much chemotherapy to mouse brains as unactivated carriers. Near infrared light propels the tiny carriers, while attached DNA helps them latch onto cells that ferry them across the blood brain barrier. In mice with glioblastoma, the treatment slowed tumor growth and extended survival with less toxicity than conventional methods. Oxford neuroscientist Tim Viney told the Financial Times the evidence needs significant improvement and questioned whether it will work in humans.

And finally, Zoox is building a street vocabulary for its robotaxis, starting with a gentler way to say watch out. In tests with Virginia Tech, forty participants, half with low vision or visual impairments, completed three hundred sixty crossing trials, and custom warning tones from directional speaker arrays stopped would be jaywalkers as reliably as a horn, without the jarring noise. The tests used a closed course with a safety driver, not Zoox robotaxis. The Rundown says quiet electric robotaxis need a way to speak up, especially for blind and low vision pedestrians, though teaching them to say go ahead is harder.

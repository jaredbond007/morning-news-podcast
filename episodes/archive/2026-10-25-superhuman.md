October twenty fifth, twenty twenty six. The Superhuman newsletter. Hair growth pill passes key trial.

First, US fusion company Marathon claims it produced stable gold by bombarding mercury with neutrons, beating CERN's record tenfold. The method fires neutrons from fusion reactions at mercury, creating an unstable form that decays into gold. The claim has not been independently verified, but it set off a frenzy on social media, with many wondering whether lab made gold could crash gold prices.

Next, California startup Alef marked what it says is its one thousandth test flight by flying its car over a Tesla Roadster, declaring it has been in the flying car race for eleven years. Unlike Tesla's brief thruster assisted jumps, Alef's three hundred thousand dollar Model A appears to use electric propulsion for sustained flight. Unrestricted customer use still requires further regulatory approval.

Then, Veradermics says its extended release oral minoxidil pill produced significantly more hair growth than a placebo after six months in more than five hundred men with pattern baldness. The pill was generally well tolerated, with no treatment related serious side effects reported. The key commercial question is whether patients will pay a premium over cheap generic minoxidil.

And, startup Radical Numerics says it used its Omnii AI model to customize bacteria killing viruses, called phages, for a patient whose E. coli infection resisted antibiotics and existing phage treatments. The FDA reportedly authorized the trial. If it works, the approach could produce personalized treatments for drug resistant infections within days, but whether it actually works remains unknown.

In good news, Cambridge researchers developed a ten dollar optical sensor that identifies substances by how they interact with light, which could put a miniature medical lab on your wrist. In tests it classified foods, medicines and plastics with one hundred percent accuracy and measured glucose, alcohol and lactate, though much larger clinical studies are needed.

And finally, an experimental treatment that reprograms a patient's immune cells has helped people with stiff person syndrome, a rare autoimmune disease, regain movement and sharply reduce falls. One participant who relied on a wheelchair can now walk around her home and work in her yard. The improvements have lasted two years, but larger studies are still needed to confirm safety and long term effectiveness.

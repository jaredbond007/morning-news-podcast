September seventeenth, twenty twenty six. The Rundown Robotics newsletter. Agility's new safer humanoid.

Agility Robotics has unveiled Digit five, a humanoid standing five foot eleven and weighing two hundred eighty four pounds, built to work next to people without safety cages. Sensors and onboard AI track nearby people, and the robot steers around them, stops, or drops into a seated position and cuts motor power when someone gets too close. It can lift fifty pounds, reach shelves seven point two feet high, and get ninety minutes of runtime from a nine minute charge, which Agility says allows more than twenty hours of work a day. Early access starts in the first half of twenty twenty seven, and Agility says it already has more than three hundred million dollars in multi year orders. The catch is the business itself, since filings show just one point seven eight million dollars in twenty twenty five sales against a one hundred forty million dollar operating loss, ahead of its two and a half billion dollar SPAC merger.

Waymo plans to launch fully driverless taxis in Tokyo in twenty twenty seven, its first commercial robotaxi service in Asia. It is partnering with the taxi app giant GO and Nihon Kotsu, a taxi operator nearly a century old, starting with a small fleet and scaling to about one hundred vehicles. Waymo began driving in Tokyo in twenty twenty five and has spent the past year adapting to left hand traffic, narrow streets and dense crowds. Regulatory approval is still pending, and Nissan, Wayve, Uber and Nuro are all chasing Tokyo robotaxi plans too. Japan's severe driver shortage gives the service a practical reason to exist.

Unitree became one of China's biggest humanoid names by driving costs sharply lower, but a slump after its IPO is raising questions about whether cheap hardware makes a durable business, Ars Technica reports. Founder Wang Xingxing reportedly weighs in on everything from product design to expense reimbursements over fifteen dollars. That cost obsession lets Unitree sell its G one from thirteen thousand five hundred dollars and its newer R one for just four thousand nine hundred. Employees say the tradeoff was quality, with high repair return rates on early robots, and most customers are still universities and research labs rather than large industrial buyers.

UBTECH has opened a Chinese factory designed to build more than ten thousand humanoids a year, with a ten minute production cycle. The plant mainly builds its Walker S and Cruzr robots, with humanoids already handling palletizing and material loading on the line, and Siemens software running a full digital twin of the factory. UBTECH sold nine hundred twenty one humanoids in the first half of twenty twenty six for eighty eight million dollars in revenue, but still lost about forty eight million. Figure, Agility and 1X are building similar capacity, so the industry is installing mass production well ahead of mass adoption.

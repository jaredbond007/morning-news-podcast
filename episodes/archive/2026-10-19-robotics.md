October nineteenth, twenty twenty six. The Rundown Robotics newsletter. SoftBank's eighteen billion dollar bet on robot guards.

First, SoftBank is reportedly in early talks to buy Allied Universal, America's largest security services company, at an equity value of up to eighteen billion dollars, with Masayoshi Son eyeing its guard posts as future robot jobs. Allied staffs offices, venues, events and New York City's subway, and has a growing data center business. Talks are preliminary with no price agreed, and adding Allied's roughly twelve billion dollars in debt could push the deal near thirty billion. Son reportedly sees security as a test bed for robotics from SoftBank's companies and would eventually replace some staff with robots. The deal would sit alongside SoftBank's agreement last year to buy ABB's robotics business for five point four billion dollars, and with about seven hundred seventy thousand employees in more than one hundred countries, Allied could give SoftBank a vast customer network for future security robots.

Next, Munich's Devanthro renamed itself Plim Robotics and debuted its first home robot, a wheeled helper that does chores on its own until a faraway relative takes over. In its step in mode, a person sees through the robot's cameras, controls its movements and speaks through it, with their face shown on its visor. It stands five foot five, weighs one hundred seventy six pounds, rolls on a three wheel base, handles objects with two finger grippers and runs on Nvidia's Jetson Thor, though it lists just two hours of battery life. Plim says it has worked in real homes since twenty twenty four, starting in elderly care, but has no price, delivery date or published performance data.

Then, two Chicago lawsuits accuse Coco Robotics and Serve Robotics of collecting biometric data without consent, alleging their delivery robots scan customers' faces and hands during pickups and keep the data for AI training. Illinois' Biometric Information Privacy Act generally requires written notice and consent first. Coco says it has collected no biometric data in Chicago, and Serve says its systems do not identify individuals or build biometric profiles. Damages run one thousand dollars per negligent violation and five thousand per reckless one, and Chicago's robot delivery pilot expires in May twenty twenty seven unless officials act.

And finally, European researchers in the PIPEON project, backed by nearly nine million dollars in EU funding through twenty twenty eight, are developing tiny wheeled robots that combine cameras, laser mapping and acoustic sensors to inspect aging sewer pipes and spot defects before costly spills. The team also plans robotic arms to clear blockages and install monitoring equipment. The prototype still uses a game controller, and researchers estimate two to three years before robots can travel between manholes on their own. In January, a collapsed sewer line outside Washington, D C dumped more than two hundred forty million gallons of raw wastewater into the Potomac.

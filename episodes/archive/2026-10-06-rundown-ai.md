October sixth, twenty twenty six. The Rundown AI newsletter. America's newest answer to China's open AI.

First, Reflection AI has entered the open model race. The twenty five billion dollar startup founded by former DeepMind researchers raised nearly five billion dollars over two years without releasing a public model, and has now introduced Beam, an open weight model for coding and agents. Reflection says Beam matches the Chinese lab Z dot ai's GLM five point two on reasoning and coding while using a fraction of the computing power, and beats Thinking Machines' Inkling and other American open models in its own tests, though Moonshot's Kimi K three still leads on raw ability. It plans to release the weights this month under a permissive license so companies can customize and run Beam themselves, with a longer term pitch of AI factories where a hedge fund or government runs it on its own chips and private data, reportedly being tested in South Korea. The Rundown notes Beam's main comparison is a Chinese model that has already been replaced, a sign of how far ahead the Chinese labs still are.

Next, Andreessen Horowitz released the seventh edition of its top one hundred consumer AI apps, adding a new ranking by American card spending. ChatGPT has three times the paid subscribers of its rivals, while Claude caught up to Gemini this summer. Paying for AI is still rare, with only four and a half percent of Americans subscribing to a major chatbot, but the top one percent average nine hundred three dollars a month and outspend the bottom half, mostly using AI as a work tool. Subscriptions dominate, with eighty four percent of the list's web apps selling one and just fourteen percent running ads. Personal agents are the new front, with Meta's Muse reportedly passing five million downloads in under a month.

Then, OpenAI introduced textGrain, a hidden watermark coming to ChatGPT and Codex text across the European Union in the next few weeks to meet the AI Act's labeling rules, with API customers elsewhere able to switch it on. It leaves a pattern invisible to readers but readable by a detector, and OpenAI says it does not affect quality or speed. Anthropic launched its own Claude watermarks in August using Google's SynthID, and OpenAI claims textGrain matched or beat it in testing. The detector is open only to vetted researchers at first, and OpenAI spelled out what a watermark cannot prove, like who wrote the words or whether they are true. The Rundown notes Anthropic faced backlash for putting its watermark on every Claude product worldwide, so OpenAI is treating this more like compliance than something it fully endorses.

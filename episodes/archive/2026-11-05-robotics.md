November fifth, twenty twenty six. The Rundown Robotics newsletter. Agility's two point five billion dollar humanoid hits Nasdaq.

First, Agility Robotics, maker of the tote hauling humanoid Digit, lists on Nasdaq today as AGLT, the first US humanoid pure play, valued at two point five billion dollars before the money on just one point eight million dollars of twenty twenty five revenue. Digit five, unveiled in September, stands five foot eleven, lifts fifty pounds repeatedly and recharges in nine minutes, enough for more than twenty hours of work a day, and Agility says it is the first humanoid evaluated in the field against OSHA standards. The company went public through a SPAC merger with Churchill Capital Corp eleven, expected to bring four hundred sixty five million dollars before expenses. Agility lost one hundred thirty eight point one million dollars in twenty twenty five, and five robots bought by a single investor made up over half its revenue. Its biggest contract covers one thousand robots over three years, its Salem, Oregon plant is designed to build ten thousand a year, and it plans to bring Digit five to the EU and UK in twenty twenty seven.

Next, Apple's first home robot is reportedly slipping to twenty twenty seven or later, The Information reports, after about two years in development. The hardware, code named J five ninety five, is reportedly mostly done, a nine inch screen on a swiveling robotic arm that turns toward people during calls. The hard part is software, since Apple wants it to understand who and what is around it, act on its own and control parts of the smart home. It is targeted at around one thousand dollars or more, and software delays could push it into twenty twenty eight. Ars Technica calls the reported features underwhelming next to existing smart home gear, making that price a hard sell.

Then, Beijing barred SoftBank from buying ABB's robotics business in China, the unit's biggest market, blocking the China part of the five point four billion dollar deal. Regulators reportedly feared data from its factory robots could leave the country and train AI models abroad, including OpenAI's, where SoftBank is a major investor. China makes up about thirty percent of ABB's robotics business, and ABB runs a one hundred fifty million dollar factory and research center in Shanghai. After China's intervention in Meta's Manus takeover in April, future robotics deals involving Chinese operations could face similar scrutiny over who gets the data.

And finally, Xiaomi posted a video of two full size humanoids logging eight cumulative hours sorting car parts at its EV factory in China, its longest published factory run so far. The robots move console side covers from bins into racks, and Xiaomi says they can correct anomalies on their own. But Xiaomi has not disclosed how many parts were sorted, the success rate or how often humans stepped in. For comparison, Figure says its Figure zero two worked ten hour shifts at BMW and loaded more than ninety thousand parts.
